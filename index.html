SHADE-LA is about shade inequality in Los Angeles and how access to urban shade reflects deeper systemic inequalities across the city. While often overlooked, the presence—or absence—of trees, canopies, and shaded infrastructure reveals patterns tied to income, race, and environmental planning. This project highlights how something as simple as shade can shape public health, comfort, and opportunity in profoundly unequal ways.

Los Angeles is one of the sunniest cities in the U.S., but relief from the heat is not shared equally. Tree canopy cover in affluent neighborhoods can be 40–60% higher than in historically redlined or low-income areas, making some streets 10–15°F cooler than others. This disparity affects health, energy bills, and public comfort, turning something as simple as shade into a marker of environmental privilege.

This map shows where shade exists across Los Angeles. Wealthier areas like the Westside and Pasadena feature dense tree canopies and shaded streets, while neighborhoods in South LA and the Eastside remain largely exposed. Shade coverage directly correlates with historic investment and urban planning decisions.

Extreme heat doesn’t hit every neighborhood the same. Urban heat islands form in low-shade, high-pavement areas, where surfaces absorb and radiate heat. These regions can feel 10–20°F hotter, amplifying health risks and making outdoor life uncomfortable for residents without reliable cooling.

Energy costs rise with heat exposure. Low-income households in high-heat neighborhoods often spend a higher percentage of income on air conditioning, while shaded, wealthier areas naturally stay cooler. This reinforces economic inequity, as families with the least resources face the highest energy burdens.

Environmental inequality extends to health outcomes. Neighborhoods with less shade and higher heat exposure overlap with lower life expectancy rates. Chronic heat stress, combined with limited green space, contributes to long-term health challenges in under-resourced communities.

Shade scarcity often overlaps with poorer air quality. Freeways, industrial zones, and treeless streets combine to trap smog and particulate matter, creating hot, polluted microclimates. These conditions disproportionately impact respiratory health and reduce overall livability.
